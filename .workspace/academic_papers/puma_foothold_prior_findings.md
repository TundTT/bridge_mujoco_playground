# PUMA: Perception-driven Unified Foothold Prior for Mobility Augmented Quadruped Parkour

**Authors:** Liang Wang, Kanzhong Yao, Yang Liu, Weikai Qin, Jun Wu, Zhe Sun, Qiuguo Zhu
**Year:** 2026 (arXiv 2601.15995v1, submitted 22 Jan 2026)

## Summary

PUMA is an end-to-end single-stage RL framework that fuses onboard depth camera observations with proprioception to estimate *egocentric polar foothold priors* — compact 4-scalar motion guidance vectors (relative distances and heading angles to safe footholds) that are fed directly into a PPO actor network. Instead of enforcing explicit foothold tracking, footholds act as soft motion priors, allowing the policy to freely adapt body posture while still benefiting from geometric terrain awareness. The framework achieves state-of-the-art success on challenging parkour tasks including stepping stones, wide-gap wall-assisted jumps, and platform surmounting, with full zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Relevant Findings for the 0.1m Bridge Problem

- **Egocentric polar foothold prior is far superior to Cartesian foothold representations.** The foothold prior is expressed as `f_t = {d^(L), d^(R), psi_t, psi_{t+1}}` — left/right Euclidean distances from front feet to the nearest safe foothold, plus current and next heading errors. This polar decomposition achieves significantly lower regression MSE (6.33% on wall-gap terrain vs. 12.10% for explicit Cartesian prior, 9.30% for implicit Cartesian prior). For a bridge-crossing agent, encoding the *lateral distance to the edge* explicitly in this polar form could give the policy the precise, low-noise signal it needs to keep feet on a 10 cm surface.

- **Safety-filtered candidate foothold set uses edge distance thresholding.** Valid footholds are defined as `P = {p_i | d_edge(p_i) > d_safe}`, where `d_edge` is the Euclidean distance to the nearest terrain edge. On a 10 cm bridge, nearly all naively sampled footholds would be filtered out — but this same mechanism can be inverted: footholds could be biased *toward* the bridge centerline by selecting candidates whose `d_edge` is maximized (i.e., closest to center). This gives the policy an explicit "stay centred" prior rather than hoping it emerges from reward alone.

- **Footholds sampled along commanded direction at 1 m intervals; front feet only.** The system focuses the foothold prior exclusively on the two front feet, reducing complexity. For the narrow-bridge problem this is directly applicable: generate foothold candidates along the bridge centerline at fixed spacing and provide the front-foot lateral offset to centerline as a polar observation. This is a minimal, low-noise signal that avoids the sparse-heightmap ambiguity at 3 cm/cell resolution.

- **PAS (Probability Annealing Selection) curriculum for foothold input.** During training the actor probabilistically receives either ground-truth foothold coordinates (from simulation) or the regression estimator's prediction, with the probability of using ground truth decaying as `p_t = 1 - cos(pi*t / 2T)`. This allows the policy to first learn *what to do with* a perfect foothold signal before having to cope with estimation error. For the 0.1m bridge, starting with ground-truth centerline offsets as an auxiliary observation and annealing toward the heightmap-derived estimate could bootstrap learning where pure heightmap exploration fails entirely.

- **Multi-critic (MuC) architecture separates task, foothold-guidance, and regularization reward streams.** Each critic group gets its own advantage estimate; these are normalized and combined for policy updates. Removing MuC caused success rates to drop from 96.8% to 89.0% on surmounting at 80° and from 96.2% to 90.9% on wall-gap tasks. The key insight: foothold guidance rewards and task rewards have conflicting gradients during dynamic manoeuvres, and a single critic fails to balance them. On the bridge, adding a dedicated "lateral centering" critic stream (separate from forward-progress reward) may prevent the policy from sacrificing foot placement quality for speed.

- **Foothold reward design: dense + sparse components.** `r^dense = exp(-(d^L + d^R))` rewards proximity of both front feet to target foothold; `r^sparse = 1(d^L < epsilon AND d^R < epsilon)` gives a bonus only when *both* feet land within threshold simultaneously. Ablations show removing the foothold prior entirely degrades Stepping Stones SR from 98.7% to 69.6%. Translating to bridge crossing: a dense reward for lateral distance of all four feet from bridge centreline, plus a sparse reward for all four feet simultaneously within bridge half-width, could provide the learning signal currently absent at 0.1m.

- **Body posture adaptation is an emergent consequence of foothold guidance, not an explicit reward.** The paper shows that access to foothold location information causes the robot to autonomously learn body reorientation (roll/pitch adjustments) to establish contact with inclined walls — this posture adaptation emerges instrumentally from the foothold signal, not from a pose reward. On a 10 cm bridge, providing explicit centerline-offset foothold priors may similarly cause the policy to discover a tightrope-like gait (e.g., tandem or diagonal foot placement) without needing to hard-code such gaits.

- **Ablation: removing relative distance component (keeping only yaw/heading) causes 0% success on wall-gap at 80° inclination.** The distance component of the polar prior is essential for tasks requiring precise lateral foot placement, not just directional guidance. This directly mirrors the bridge problem: heading alone is insufficient — the policy needs an explicit lateral distance-to-safe-region signal.

- **Depth image encoded via CNN + self-attention + GRU for temporal smoothing.** The architecture processes a depth image buffer through CNN, self-attention over proprioceptive + visual history, then a GRU for temporal modeling before regressing the foothold prior. A single-frame heightmap may be too noisy for 10 cm targets; a GRU-smoothed depth history could reduce effective noise in edge detection.

- **Stepping stones curriculum: stone width decreases as difficulty increases.** The paper's curriculum reduces stepping stone size progressively, validating the bridge-width curriculum approach already in use. The key missing ingredient appears to be the foothold prior that guides foot placement onto narrowing surfaces — not just curriculum scheduling alone.

## Recommendation

Add an explicit egocentric polar foothold prior observation to the bridge-crossing policy: compute the signed lateral distance from each front foot to the bridge centerline (equivalently, distance to nearest bridge edge) and express it as a 2-scalar observation fed directly to the actor, initially as ground truth from simulation geometry and annealed toward heightmap-derived estimates using PAS (start at 100% ground truth, decay to 0% over ~8K training steps). Pair this with a dedicated foothold-guidance reward stream using a dense centerline-proximity term `exp(-(|y^L_foot| + |y^R_foot|))` and a sparse term for all four feet within bridge half-width, run through a separate critic head so it does not compete with the forward-progress reward gradient — this directly addresses the 0.1m failure mode where the 3 cm/cell heightmap cannot provide the sub-centimetre lateral precision needed and pure reward exploration cannot discover valid foot placements.
